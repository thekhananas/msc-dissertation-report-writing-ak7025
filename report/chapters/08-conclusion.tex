\chapter{Conclusion}
\label{ch:conclusion}

The dissertation examined whether additional programming evidence helps performance prediction, and how a system should handle
that evidence when it may be unreliable. The four research questions produced the following answers.

\paragraph{RQ1: Extra coding evidence.}
On the corrected authored benchmark, executable evidence changed accuracy from 18 to 19 correct predictions among 23 eligible
cases. The accuracy difference was 4.35 percentage points. Its paired 95\% interval of zero to 13.04 percentage points includes the declared ten-point useful improvement, so that benefit
remains unresolved. Passing unrelated and relevant evidence produced the same decisions. The earlier, larger apparent effect
depended on an execution-harness defect, showing why executable assessment needs its own scrutiny.

\paragraph{RQ2: Limiting a bad check.}
Across the tracker simulator's adverse settings, bounded updates reduced average Brier error compared with ordinary Bayesian updates using the assumed evidence model. They met the
declared penalty limit under clean evidence. Bounded updates increased error under missing and contradictory evidence. The guarantee limits one
observation's contribution to log-odds. Repeated misleading observations can still accumulate.

\paragraph{RQ3: Choosing checks.}
When half the cases received a probe, the candidate beat random selection but lost to uncertainty-based selection and the plug-in
clipped-belief rule. Average error across the held-out settings increased as probe use rose. The acquisition score need not equal
the expected error reduction of the executed decision, so this result concerns the implemented heuristic and does not answer
whether ordinary value-of-information selection would succeed. At full budget, differences concern updating rather than ranking.

\paragraph{RQ4: Finding likely errors.}
The exploratory CSEDM study used 729 predictions from 86 learners across 19 problems. Selecting 361 predictions by uncertainty
captured 141 of 195 model errors (72.3\%), compared with 49.5\% expected under random selection. No corrective review was performed, so the
analysis does not show that selection would correct errors or improve later performance. The confidence interval for the difference in error recall holds the fitted
predictions and selections fixed. It does not establish transfer to new problems.

\paragraph{Contribution and implication.}
The pipeline preserves predictions before outcome reveal and exposes failures in both executable assessment and evidence use.
An AI tutor should validate the check and the decision rule before changing a performance prediction. The working tutor
illustrates that application. Teaching effectiveness and learning gains require a human study.

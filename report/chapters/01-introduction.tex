\chapter{Introduction}
\label{ch:introduction}

In this dissertation, an AI programming tutor means an interactive system that uses artificial intelligence to help a learner
work through programming problems through questions, explanations or hints. This working definition draws on research on
intelligent tutoring and conversational tutoring systems \parencite{vanlehn2011relative,nye2014autotutor}. The term describes
the intended educational role. Evidence of effective teaching depends on how the system is evaluated.

Current products illustrate this setting. Khan Academy describes Khanmigo as a tutor that guides learners through problems,
while Codecademy's AI Learning Assistant provides code explanations and hints within programming exercises
\parencite{khanacademyKhanmigo,codecademyLearningAssistant}. General language models can also support tutoring through a
configured interface, as in the GPT-based systems studied by Bastani and colleagues \parencite{bastani2025guardrails}.
Anthropic's Claude Learning mode similarly uses questions to guide reasoning \parencite{anthropic2025education}.
These examples establish the application context. The product descriptions do not establish independent learning gains or
show that these products use the evidence-selection method investigated here.

An adaptive programming tutor must decide whether to advance, ask another question, offer a hint, or request a short coding
check. A fluent explanation can sound convincing while failing to show whether the person can solve a related task alone.
Code offers another source of evidence, yet the task may be poorly chosen, the tests may miss part of the requirement, or the
result may concern a different skill. An extra check can therefore improve a prediction or make it less reliable.

This dissertation studies that decision in the setting of an AI programming tutor. It asks how a system should use extra coding
evidence when predicting performance on a later task. The working tutoring interface illustrates a Socratic interaction.
The experiments assess prediction and evidence use. Teaching effectiveness remains a question for a future student study.

\section{Why Study the Evidence Decision?}

Success with assistance can differ from independent performance. In a high-school mathematics study, Bastani and colleagues
compared a general-purpose GPT-based interface, a tutor with teacher-informed guardrails, and a control without generative AI.
Both AI conditions improved assisted practice performance. On the subsequent unassisted exam, the general-purpose condition
performed worse than the control. The guarded tutor did not show a statistically detectable advantage over the control
\parencite{bastani2025guardrails}. The result concerns those tools and that educational setting, rather than showing that all
AI assistance harms learning.

PISA 2025 adds broader educational context. The OECD reports that associations between AI use and science performance vary
with the purpose of use and opportunities to develop AI literacy: students reporting AI use for specific schoolwork activities
scored lower on average, while weekly general use was associated with similar performance to non-use
\parencite{oecd2026pisa2025}. These are observational associations. Prior attainment and the
reasons students seek assistance are possible alternative explanations. Published during the writing of this report, the
findings reinforce the relevance of assessing performance carefully when AI is available.

This motivates assessing what can be done independently, rather than treating a successful assisted answer as sufficient
evidence. The present dissertation examines an earlier engineering question: how should a system interpret evidence when
predicting a separate task outcome? Its external-model benchmark contains no student intervention, and its later coding task
is an assessment of the evaluation model under fixed prompts. It cannot reproduce the human-learning result above or measure
whether Socratic guidance reduces dependence on assistance.

A tutor that accepts a convincing answer too readily may move on before a difficulty has been resolved. Repeated checks also
have a burden: they require another response and, for executable work, another run of the code. The useful question is whether
that extra observation supports a better decision. A low execution cost alone cannot answer it. An incomplete test or a poorly
interpreted result may provide little help, even when running it is cheap.

The original project proposed a broader Socratic tutor with a model of learner state. The completed work isolates the evidence
and prediction problem so that its assumptions can be inspected. In the external-model study, an evaluation model supplies
answers and code under fixed prompts. The simulations make evidence faults controllable,
and the historical learner records provide a separate test of uncertainty-based error selection. Each setting answers a
different part of the problem.

The corrected external-model benchmark found one additional correct prediction among 23 eligible cases, an improvement of
4.35 percentage points. Its 95\% paired bootstrap interval ranged from 0 to 13.04 points, including the prespecified
10-point useful difference. The study therefore left a useful benefit unresolved. The interval also has the small-sample and
case-dependence limitations discussed in \cref{ch:evaluation}.

This uncertain finding motivated the later investigation, but did not prove that selective acquisition would solve the problem. The
tracker maps evidence categories to fixed score changes. Two different checks can consequently lead to the same prediction.
The follow-up studies therefore examine both the handling of uncertain evidence and the allocation of checks, while retaining
the limits of the initial instrument. They do not establish actual financial savings or a benefit to learning.

\section{Research Questions}

The central question is:

\begin{quote}
When a programming tutor can request an extra coding check, when does that check give useful evidence about a later task, and
when can it make the prediction less reliable?
\end{quote}

In this report, an executable check is a separate short programming task whose code is run against authored tests. The
acquisition experiment studies a narrower case: it gives exactly 20 of 40 simulated cases an extra check, then compares rules
for choosing those 20 cases. It does not decide whether to spend the allocation, adapt after each selection, or measure money
or classroom time.

The four study questions are stated below using the corrected method descriptions. The frozen plans retain their original
wording and policy identifiers. The original RQ3 named ordinary value-of-information selection as a comparator. The implemented
comparator instead scores changes in probability after limiting the effect of evidence. This score can favour a check even
when its result would leave the predicted outcome unchanged. That part of the original question remains unanswered. RQ3 below describes
the comparison actually performed. This wording was clarified after the experiment had been frozen.
No predictions, outcomes, or implemented comparators were changed by the clarification.

The study sequence also matters. The RQ1--RQ3 narrative was recorded on 4 September 2026, after the earlier benchmark and tracker
work. Their original protocols retain their own dates. The student records come from the 2019 Computer Science Educational
Data Mining (CSEDM) data challenge. The project's analysis specification for these records is dated 10 September 2026.
Its configuration matches the recorded prediction run, but the available evidence has yet to establish when results were first
inspected. The study is therefore reported as exploratory. The acquisition interpretation was corrected on 11 September.
These dates describe how the investigation developed.

\begin{description}
  \item[RQ1: Extra coding evidence.] When every case receives an extra coding check, does that check improve the system's
  prediction of whether the evaluation model will solve a different Python task? Predictions are saved before the later task
  is requested.
  \item[RQ2: Limiting a bad check.] In the declared simulator, does limiting the effect of one check improve prediction quality
  when checks are unreliable, while keeping any loss small when checks are reliable?
  \item[RQ3: Choosing checks.] When only half of the cases can receive an extra check, does the proposed rule choose better cases
  than random choice, choosing the most uncertain cases, or a simpler rule that uses estimated reliability?
  \item[RQ4: Finding likely errors.] In historical CSEDM records, does selecting the most uncertain predictions identify more
  errors in predictions of first-attempt success than selecting the same number of predictions at random?
\end{description}

RQ4 asks whether uncertainty helps locate prediction errors in historical learner records. Those records contain the outcomes
needed to assess error selection. Assessing the probe policy would also require outcomes from optional checks, which these
records do not contain. The direct answers appear in \cref{ch:evaluation}. Their limits are examined in \cref{ch:discussion}.

\section{Contribution}

The engineering contribution is a reproducible pipeline that separates visible evidence, fixed predictions, and withheld
outcomes, with recorded execution and correction histories. The methodological work implements and tests bounded evidence
updates and a reliability-aware fixed-budget selection rule. Bayesian updating and expected-loss decision theory are established
methods. The contribution lies in implementing the specified rules and examining their behaviour under faulty evidence.

The main selection result is negative. The proposed rule beat random choice, yet it made more errors on average across the
six test environments than choosing the most uncertain cases or using a simpler estimate of check reliability. Requesting
more checks also increased the average error. A mathematical review after the experiment found that the rule could favour
a check even when either result would leave the predicted outcome unchanged. Its score therefore need not represent a
reduction in prediction error. This limits what the comparison can establish about ordinary value-of-information selection.

Together, the studies examine how assessment defects, evidence assumptions, and decision rules affect measured performance.
Their contribution is bounded by the chosen tasks and environments. They do not establish improved teaching, realistic student
simulation, or an optimal evidence-acquisition policy. Table~\ref{tab:contributions} summarises what each part contributes
and the limits of its evidence.

The contribution is therefore an inspectable evaluation system and a set of bounded empirical and methodological findings.
The tutoring demonstration makes that system's context tangible, but is not an additional effectiveness study. Any claim of

\cref{ch:related-work}.

\section{Report Structure}

\Cref{ch:related-work} examines the work that informed the assessment and decision methods.
\Cref{ch:measurement} defines the authored benchmark and the separation between evidence used for prediction and the later
outcome used for evaluation. \Cref{ch:system} explains how the implementation preserves that separation and records failures.

\Cref{ch:method} describes the Bayesian tracker, the fixed-budget acquisition heuristic, and the separate historical-data
study. \Cref{ch:evaluation} presents their results, including the execution-harness correction and the unsuccessful acquisition
comparison. \Cref{ch:discussion} considers alternative explanations and the limits of applying these findings to tutoring.
\Cref{ch:conclusion} states what the dissertation establishes and what remains untested.

\chapter{Measurement Design}
\label{ch:measurement}

The first study asked whether an extra coding check improved a prediction of success on a separate task. The language model
\texttt{gpt-oss-120b} supplied answers and code for 24 authored Python cases covering four concepts and eight misconceptions.
Each case began with an answer that reviewers could read. A related coding task supplied additional evidence. The system
also formed controls by substituting unrelated evidence or reversing test results. A separate coding task, called the criterion
task, supplied the outcome used to assess the predictions. The workflow saved predictions before requesting this final code
response. Its ordering checks are described in Chapter~\ref{ch:system}.

\section{Case Construction}

The corpus covers assignment evaluation, object aliasing, function arguments and conditional control. Each concept contains
two authored misconception groups, with three cases per group. Examples include treating assignment as copying a mutable
object, assuming that a function copies its argument, and treating a conditional as two independent checks. These groups
define the intended coverage. Their frequency in the corpus does not estimate prevalence among students.

Each case links a public prompt to an executable evidence task and a separate criterion task. The manifest records their
paths and hashes, an initial tracker state, control assignments, and evaluator-only review and structural-difference records.
The structural records explain how the evidence and criterion tasks differ. For example, one evidence task asks for the sorted
result after adding a permission, while its criterion asks for a removal operation through a function over a different
collection. Differences in operation, signature and values reduce literal answer reuse. They do not by themselves validate
educational transfer or exclude shared shortcuts.

The manifest also retains authored evidence-pattern labels used during benchmark construction. These are design metadata,
not observed performance labels for the external model. Its public response is rated independently, and its criterion outcome
comes from executing its submitted code. Neither outcome is inferred from the intended case pattern. Calling the corpus
held-out refers to its experimental role. It does not establish that these concepts or similar questions were absent from
the model's training data.

\section{Control Construction}

The unrelated control uses a preassigned source case whose target-concept label differs from the target case. It copies that
source's recorded evidence pass and fail counts unchanged. The implementation rejects self-assignment and same-concept
assignment, and records the source summary and assignment hashes. The mapping is fixed rather than selected after inspecting
which source gives a favourable comparison. Different concept labels define unrelated evidence for this experiment.
The underlying programming skills may still overlap.

The corrupted control swaps the target evidence's pass and fail counts. All-pass evidence becomes all-fail evidence, and
vice versa. A mixed result remains in the conflicting category after the swap, so corruption does not necessarily change
the tracker's update for every possible input. The recorded seed is provenance metadata. This transformation is deterministic
and does not draw random flips. It changes the summary supplied to the tracker, not the submitted program or the sandbox.

Both controls reuse existing evidence summaries. They require no extra model generation and are not separate tutor
conversations. This design isolates the response of the fixed tracker to substituted evidence. It cannot show how a live
tutor or student would react to receiving an unrelated question or misleading feedback.

\section{From Observations to Predictions}

The evaluation model answered the public, evidence and criterion prompts in separate contexts. Here, isolated means that a
response did not receive the conversation history from another channel. It does not mean that the model had never encountered
similar tasks during training. The later criterion was withheld from the tracker and prediction process. Its completion tests
model performance rather than learning caused by the earlier prompt.

The v1 tracker is an authored additive rule, separate from the Bayesian tracker introduced later. It starts at a score of 0.5.
A correct observation adds 0.2. An incorrect answer or misconception subtracts 0.2. A conflicting observation subtracts 0.1,
while an uncertain or empty observation leaves the score unchanged. Each update is restricted to the interval $[0,1]$:
\begin{equation}
  s^{+}=\min(1,\max(0,s+\Delta_c)),
\end{equation}
where $\Delta_c$ is the fixed change for the observation category. The stored field is named
\texttt{mastery\_probability}, but the score is not a calibrated probability or a measurement of human mastery. Recorded
confidence values do not affect this update. The binary prediction is success when the final score is at least 0.6.

Independent public-answer ratings provide the first observation. Each condition then starts from an identical copy of the
post-answer state. Dialogue alone receives no further update. The other conditions receive the category derived from their
respective test counts: all checks passing means correct, all failing means incorrect, and a mixture means conflicting.
An empty set of test results is an invalid input. The conditions are evaluated separately. Updates from
one condition cannot carry into another.

This rule deliberately compresses the evidence. It does not inspect program meaning or weight a result by its relevance to
the target concept. Two checks with the same outcome category therefore produce the same update, even when one is unrelated.
The unrelated control tests this limitation of the chosen prediction rule. Equality between those conditions cannot establish
that relevance never matters for a richer tracker.

\section{Public-Answer Review}

Two reviewers assessed the 24 public responses: an MSc Computing student and a senior software engineer. Each received a CSV
containing the case identifier, public prompt, visible response and response hash, together with the shared category guide.
The packet excluded executable probes, test results, criterion material, authored evidence patterns and condition predictions.
Here, blinded refers to those withheld materials. It does not imply that every aspect of the study or model identity was hidden.
No case-specific answer key was supplied.

Reviewers were instructed to enter a category and a short rationale for each response, without changing the packet fields.
The guide applies categories in this order: empty, conflicting, uncertain, correct, misconception, then incorrect. Empty means
there is no assessable answer. Conflicting means the response commits to incompatible answers or explanations. Uncertain means
it offers only doubt or a guess without committing. Correct requires both the answer and its explanation to follow from the
visible prompt. Misconception identifies a wrong answer supported by a specific false rule. Incorrect covers a wrong answer
without enough explanation to identify such a rule. This precedence prevents a correct final line from automatically overriding
an earlier incompatible commitment. The guide supplies fixed confidence values, but the v1 tracker ignores that field.

The author-recorded procedure states that the reviewers completed their sheets independently, with the student completing
first. Neither saw the other's decisions, executable evidence or criterion material, and neither used additional tools.
This account was recorded after rating and before criterion access. It documents the declared procedure. The software cannot
independently establish what a reviewer saw outside the supplied packet. File hashes bind the procedure record to the submitted
ratings and guide, rather than proving reviewer independence.

The author was the planned adjudicator for genuine disagreements before predictions were sealed. The two submissions agreed
on every category, so no adjudication was required. Raw agreement and unweighted Cohen's kappa are reported in
\cref{ch:evaluation}. Agreement measures consistency of these submitted ratings. It does not validate the categories as
measurements of learning or establish complete coverage of possible response failures. The later failure review served a
different purpose and was not used to revise these public ratings.

\section{Outcomes and Paired Comparisons}

For eligible case $i$, condition $c$, binary prediction $\hat y_{ic}$, and later outcome $y_i$, classification error is

\begin{equation}
  L_{ic}=\mathbf{1}[\hat y_{ic}\neq y_i].
\end{equation}

The saved reports call the related-evidence condition ``valid probe''. This identifies its intended relation to the target
concept. It does not certify the tests or their interpretation. The paired effect comparing dialogue-only condition $d$ with
this related-evidence condition $v$ is

\begin{equation}
  \bar d=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}(L_{id}-L_{iv}),
\end{equation}

where positive values favour the additional probe. One criterion execution was missing, leaving 23 eligible pairs.

An outcome of success means that the submitted criterion program passed its authored checks. It does not establish that
the tests cover every requirement. Invalid submissions and unavailable execution outcomes remain distinct from completed
checks that fail. Neither is silently assigned a binary failure. The missing-case sensitivity appears in \cref{ch:evaluation}.

The secondary contrasts compare valid evidence with unrelated evidence and corrupted evidence separately. A combined contrast
averages those two paired differences. A positive combined value alone cannot demonstrate a relevance-specific benefit:
poorer performance under corrupted evidence can make the average positive even when valid and unrelated evidence are tied.
Cases share concepts and misconceptions, so the 23 pairs do not represent 23 independently sampled programming topics.

Figure~\ref{fig:study-design} shows when predictions and outcomes entered the workflow. Saving predictions before requesting
the later task makes it possible to check which evidence informed each decision. Chapter~\ref{ch:system} explains how the
application verifies those records and the limits of that protection.

The original sealed analysis remains part of the scientific record. A later full replay found a systematic sequence-comparison
fault in the authored test harness. The repair changed 19 of 48 execution records: 17 binary labels, one test-count record
whose binary label stayed unchanged, and one missing outcome's failure status. Chapter~\ref{ch:evaluation} compares the
original result with the complete correction and bases its interpretation on the corrected outcomes.

\section{Statistical Procedure and Interpretation}

The analysis rules developed in stages. The original specification named Brier difference, with classification error as
the fallback for uncalibrated scores, and listed McNemar among exploratory analyses. The later inferential hierarchy,
dated 1 September 2026 and required before criterion access, explicitly selected classification error and exact McNemar
for the primary comparison. It retained sign swapping and subgroup checks as secondary. This clarification followed
collection of the public and evidence responses. It therefore records a staged specification of the analysis.

The frozen analysis reports the mean paired error difference, a 95\% paired percentile bootstrap interval, and an exact
two-sided McNemar test at level 0.05. It also compares the observed mean with the declared minimum interpretable effect of
0.10 (ten percentage points). Reaching that threshold is a descriptive comparison of the mean, not proof that the true effect
exceeds it. No single significance flag replaces the effect estimate, interval and limitations.

For these fixed cases and recorded responses, the observed difference is a descriptive quantity calculated directly from
the saved outcomes. An uncertainty interval requires assumptions about what could vary. Here, resampling changes which
recorded cases contribute to the mean. It does not collect new observations or establish a sampling model for new topics.

For the bootstrap, each draw samples $N$ complete case differences with replacement and calculates their mean. Conditions
within a case stay paired. The implementation uses 10,000 draws and the 2.5th and 97.5th percentiles, with linear interpolation
between ordered draws. The corrected analysis retains seed 20260813. This procedure is conditional on the saved responses and
outcomes. It does not repeat model generation. Cases are resampled individually, so shared misconceptions and concepts are
not preserved as clusters. The interval is reported as the frozen case-resampling summary, with no claim that it provides
95\% coverage across courses, programming concepts or repeated model runs.

The percentile construction uses quantiles of the resampled statistic, as described by
\textcite{hesterberg2015bootstrap}. That source also documents small-sample undercoverage:
nominal 95\% intervals can contain the target less often than their label suggests. Its numerical examples concern particular
distributions. They do not supply a coverage estimate or a sample-size threshold for these discrete authored-case differences.

The implementation also retains its original linear quantile convention, rather than the alternative convention recommended
by Hesterberg. The citation explains the procedure and its weaknesses, without endorsing every implementation
choice. Increasing the number of resamples reduces simulation noise in the interval calculation. It does not add independent
cases or resolve weaknesses in the sampling design. The frozen interval is retained transparently, rather than replaced after
seeing the corrected outcome. A future study should choose its resampling unit and interval together with its case design.

McNemar's test uses only cases where the two predictions differ in correctness. Let $b$ count cases where dialogue is correct
and valid evidence is wrong, and let $c$ count the reverse. The implemented exact conditional two-sided value follows the
binomial formulation described by \textcite{fagerland2013mcnemar}:
\begin{equation}
  p_{\mathrm{exact}}=\min\left(1,\;2\sum_{j=0}^{\min(b,c)}
  \binom{b+c}{j}2^{-(b+c)}\right).
\end{equation}
With no discordant pairs the value is set to one. The calculation is exact under the binomial null for independent discordant
pairs. The word exact does not remove the authored-case dependence or sampling limitations. A non-significant result does
not establish equivalence. The corrected study has only one discordant pair, which limits what this test can distinguish.

The exact conditional test controls its nominal false-positive rate under its assumptions, at the cost of conservatism.
